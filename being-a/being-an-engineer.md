You are a practitioner.
Whether you're an SE I, SE II, or SE III, the core of the work is the same: ship working software, understand what you ship, learn from what you build, and invest in the people around you.
AI (artificial intelligence) coding agents now draft much of the code.
You still own all of it.
What changes as you grow is the scope of what you take on, how independently you handle it, and how much your growth lifts others.
Engineers who are supported, growing, and proud of what they ship build great software.

Our systems touch residents in hundreds of thousands of homes: the app they use to pay rent, the portal for maintenance requests, the tools associates depend on every day.
The software you ship matters, including every line an agent drafted for you.
When we get it right, people feel it.

("Residents" are the people who live in our homes; "associates" are our employees.)

David Thomas and Andrew Hunt open *The Pragmatic Programmer* with a question that applies at every level:

> *"Care about your craft.
> Why spend your life developing software unless you care about doing it well?"*
> — David Thomas and Andrew Hunt, *The Pragmatic Programmer*

No two engineers will look the same.
How you grow depends on your team, the systems you work in, and the problems in front of you, and you may move faster in some areas than others.
What stays constant: the expectation that you care about your craft, keep learning, and make the people around you better.

## Craft and Delivery

The core of this job is building software: framing the problem, specifying what you want, verifying what comes back, shipping changes, and then doing it again.
Code is cheap now.
Judgment is not.

Early on, your work is smaller and well-defined.
The goal is not just to complete the tasks.
Understand why a solution works, how it fits into the broader system, and what "done well" looks like on this team.
You're learning the codebase, the tools, the conventions, and the standard your team holds itself to.
Coding agents are part of your daily toolkit.
Use them as a tutor: ask them to explain, then check what you learned against the code and your teammates.
Don't use them as a substitute.
You should be able to explain every line you submit.
Understanding why the code works matters as much as getting it to work.

As you grow into SE II and SE III, the scope expands.
You take on larger features with less definition, contribute to design and estimation, and move toward owning implementations end-to-end.
With agents doing the drafting, expect to move faster at every level.
The scope of each level does not change, but the standard for verifying your work rises with your speed.
At SE II, you write the spec and own verifying your own work.
By SE III, you're not just executing work: you're leading it, decomposing it into small batches for people and agents, owning the design and the evals (automated checks of AI behavior and output quality), and driving it through to delivery.

At every level, the standard stays constant.
Follow the conventions your team has agreed on.
Write code that the next engineer (including future you) can understand and extend.
Test your work.
Review others' pull requests (PRs) carefully and promptly, and review agent-written changes the same way.
Review capacity, not typing speed, limits how fast we ship, so keep your batches small.
Accountability stays human: "the agent wrote it" is never an explanation for a defect or an incident.
A good solution delivered is worth more than a perfect solution in progress.

On-call and production support are part of the picture from the start.
Systems behave differently in production than in development, and your instincts sharpen fastest when you're watching real behavior.
By SE III, you're setting up postmortems, identifying root causes, turning incidents into improvements, and looking for repetitive manual work that can be automated away.

When work stalls, act.
Ask for help early.
Waiting until you're deeply stuck helps no one.
Over time, remove the small blockers yourself rather than waiting for someone else to notice.
That shift (from "I asked for help" to "I cleared the path") is one of the clearest signs of growth.

## Communication and Collaboration

Engineering is a team sport, and most of the leverage comes from how well information moves.

Good communication starts with asking questions: early, often, and without hesitation.
Every clarifying question you ask saves someone time downstream.
Listen carefully, synthesize what you hear, and write clear PR descriptions, in your own words, that give reviewers the context they need.

As you grow, the direction of information flow expands.
You're giving feedback on others' code: clearly, kindly, and with the intent to improve the work.
You're contributing to team discussions, writing documentation that others actually use, and advocating for the decisions and practices your team has agreed on.

By SE III, communication is how you create leverage for the whole team.
You're writing things down: Decision Records, diagrams, system documentation that the whole team, and its agents, can build on.
You're flagging technical risks before they become delivery problems, and asking whether you're building the right thing, not just building it right.
Knowledge that lives in your head and nowhere else is a risk to the team, and it starves the agents that work beside you.

Raise disagreements early and clearly.
Your perspective matters, and diverse viewpoints make us stronger.
Once a decision is made, commit to it fully.
Modeling that behavior (saying what you think, then getting behind the call) is how teams stay honest and still move fast.

## Learning and Curiosity

Continuously improving our craft is one of our [engineering principles](../engineering-principles).
The growth between SE I and SE III is substantial, and it does not happen passively.
When agents do the typing, staying sharp takes deliberate effort.

Be curious.
Treat every task as an opportunity to learn something: about the system, the domain, the trade-offs, or the people around you.
Stay able to build and debug without an agent.
If you could not explain the change, you are not ready to submit it.
Read and understand the architecture decisions your team has recorded, and the reasoning behind them.
Agents read them too, so keep them accurate.
When a convention seems wrong or outdated, ask about it; that is how standards evolve.

Stay curious about what is happening outside your immediate work.
The further you go, the more it matters to understand how your system fits into the broader architecture and how your decisions ripple outward.
By SE III, back those decisions with data and metrics rather than instinct alone.

Attend the Technical Architecture Guild (TAG), Solution Architecture sessions, and applicable Shared Interest Groups (SIGs).
Early on, your job is mostly to listen and build context.
Over time, bring what you learn back to your team and, eventually, contribute your own perspective to those conversations.
By SE III, sharing what you've learned (in SIGs, Monthly Learnings, or a team demo) is part of the role.

## Growing Your Reach

Our vision is to *delight our residents and associates with innovative solutions built by happy teams*.
Happy teams are made of people who invest in each other, starting from their first days on the team.
That investment looks different at each level, but it starts here.

At SE I, your primary responsibility is yourself: do the work, learn the tools, ask good questions.
At SE II, your reach begins to extend to your teammates: review their PRs, check in when someone is stuck, model a bias to action.
At SE III, your reach is the team: you lead implementations, share knowledge broadly, and invest in the growth of the engineers around you.

Mentorship at SE III is not a formal obligation.
It follows naturally from having learned enough to help others learn faster.
Pair with more junior engineers on work that will stretch them.
Agents have taken over much of the small work that used to teach people, so create those learning opportunities on purpose.
Help them use agents as tutors, and coach your pod on specs, small batches, and honest verification.
Provide the context that helps them make better decisions, not just complete tickets.
Help onboard new teammates.
Ask "why" even in challenging situations, and model the curiosity and accountability you want to see.

As your reach grows, so does your role in building the team itself.
Join interview panels and give feedback on candidates, looking for judgment more than speed.

Risk grows with autonomy.
Resident data, credentials, and production access stay out of an agent's reach unless your team has deliberately granted them.
Watch for prompt injection in content agents read, and treat tokens as a budget.
Work that touches resident data, payments, or homes deserves extra care.

The best SE IIIs are not just strong engineers.
They are the engineers people at SE I and SE II want to become.

From here, the Professional Track continues to [SE IV](being-a-software-engineer-iv) and beyond, or you may find yourself drawn toward the Management Track and the [Engineering Manager](being-an-engineering-manager) role if people leadership is where your energy goes.
What both paths require is a foundation of technical credibility, good judgment, and genuine investment in others.
That foundation is built here.
